# Improving a bias-education website for STEM workplaces

<div class="meta" markdown="1">
**Where:** UBC Social Identity Lab (Toni Schmader), 2023, volunteer · **Role:** one of a four-person UX team working with the lab's lead UX researcher; moderated sessions, ran the follow-up interviews, co-led synthesis and the readout · **Methods:** moderated remote usability tests (contextual inquiry, think-aloud), semi-structured interviews, anonymous follow-up survey, affinity mapping · **n:** 18 participants
</div>

## The decision at stake

The lab studies gender bias in STEM and how allyship counters it, and runs workshops in STEM workplaces. It had built a public website and a tool that simulates how bias compounds in a group, to carry the workshop content further. Before investing in a redesign, the principal investigator wanted to know what was unclear, what was persuasive, and for whom.

## What I did

**Sessions.** Thirty-minute video calls, one participant at a time, a moderator and a note-taker. We recruited psychology students for course credit, a free and convenient proxy for the real audience, with the caveat stated up front: students can tell you what is unclear, but STEM professionals bring workplace experience that makes content easier or harder to believe.

**Usability test, then interview.** I opened with a few minutes of conversation and set expectations, made clear the participant was not being evaluated, and then watched them navigate freely while thinking aloud. I stayed quiet unless someone lingered or struggled; then I said what I had noticed and asked what they were thinking, which pins the cause of a problem without putting one in their mouth. The interview afterward covered five things: pain points, likes and dislikes, their top three changes and why, comprehension ("how would you describe this to a friend?"), and when they would actually use the site. I closed by summarizing what I had heard so they could correct me, then sent an anonymous follow-up survey for anything they had not wanted to say to my face.

**Synthesis.** One sticky note per point raised, colored by type (likes, suggested changes, confusion, technical issues, other), then an affinity map by what each note referred to. The density of colors by area was the first read of the findings.

## What changed

Participants liked the storytelling, the graphics, the links to sources, and the navigation; they wanted more examples; some gifs moved too fast; and most did not understand why the simulator was there or what it was for. Our recommendations to the PI, the designer, and the developer:

- Embed the simulator in the main site instead of hosting it on a separate page that loaded slowly.
- Introduce the simulator earlier, with context, next to the narrative examples it is meant to demonstrate.
- Extend the narrative-and-graphic treatment to the concepts people found confusing, such as how bias affects women more even in balanced workplaces.
- Test the next version with STEM professionals, because persuasion depends on whether the content matches a reader's own workplace experience.

The layout, clarity, and navigation changes that followed cut user support complaints about threefold.
