# Pride, shame, and guilt: finding the missing variable with mixed methods

<div class="meta" markdown="1">
**Where:** University of British Columbia, doctoral research with Jessica Tracy · **Methods:** interviews, guided-recall survey, two experiments with real-time feedback, three-wave diary study · **n:** 251 (recall), 251 and 308 (experiments), 247 runners (diary)
</div>

## The problem

The leading theories said people feel pride when they succeed at something central to who they are, and shame or guilt when they fail, and that these feelings are distinct from plain happiness or sadness. Three initial studies in the moral domain found the basic pattern but not the distinctness: people felt about as proud as happy for upholding their values, and about as ashamed as sad for violating them. The theory was missing something.

## Interviews found it

I asked participants about times they had behaved morally. The answer kept coming back in the same shape: *I expect myself to do the right thing, so why would I feel proud of it?* Expectations were the missing variable. People form them from their own past behavior, from what similar people do, and from codes like laws and religion, and behaving differently from those expectations is what says something about identity. New theory: pride follows unexpected success; shame and guilt follow unexpected failure.

## Then the cheapest study that could falsify it, then harder ones

- **Guided recall (251 U.S. adults).** Because the self is different for everyone, participants first named an aspect of their identity and behaviors relevant to it, then recalled a time they had done better or worse than expected, then rated their emotions. Better-than-expected produced pride distinct from happiness; worse-than-expected produced shame and guilt distinct from sadness.
- **Real-time feedback (251 psychology majors; 308 undergraduates).** Recall is fallible, so the next studies created the moment. Students took a psychology quiz and were told their score was 10% above or below their usual grade; a second group did a fundraising task and were told they had raised more or less than others. Same pattern, measured in the moment. Everyone was debriefed, and the money raised was donated to a charity of the participant's choice.
- **Three-wave diary study (247 runners, recruited from running groups).** Expectations for the next two weeks, then the best run and the feelings about it, then new expectations, repeated. Same pattern at both time points, outside the lab. Pride raised expectations for the next period; shame and guilt lowered them. And emotions tracked people's subjective sense of how they had done relative to expectations as much as their objective metrics.

## What it shows about how I work

Use interviews to find the variable, write the theory down so it can be wrong, run the cheapest study that can falsify it, then move toward real situations and real time. The practical recommendations that came out of it, for apps and for anyone giving performance feedback: help people set realistic expectations, and when a result could sting, point to the comparison that is both true and favorable (improvement against one's own past rather than against classmates).
